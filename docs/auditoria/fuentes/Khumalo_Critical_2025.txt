Te design of underground cable systems must account for the risk of soil drying out due to heat dissipation, which can degrade cable
performance and lead to environmental concerns. Tis study investigates a cost-efective cable rating methodology tailored to South
African conditions, where native soils are used instead of engineered backfll. Using the IEC 60287 standard, an Excel-based
calculation tool is developed to assess the efects of key installation parameters, including soil thermal resistivity, ambient soil
temperature and cable laying depth. Soil samples from Sandton, South Africa, revealed thermal resistivity ranging from 0.596 K·m/W,
at 14.5% moisture, to 3.72 K·m/W, at 0% moisture, resulting in current ratings from 518.34 A to 224.21 A. Worst-case con-
ditions—high resistivity, increased depth, 1150 mm and elevated soil temperature, 28°C—reduced ampacity by over 45%. Te
fndings underscore the need to incorporate site-specifc soil data and worst-case assumptions into cable rating designs to prevent
thermal degradation. Te developed method ofers a practical, locally optimised alternative for utilities in semiarid regions.
Keywords: cable ampacity rating; electrical cable; power cable; soil drying out; soil thermal resistivity; South African conditions
1. Introduction
Te novel generation power system requires diverse energy
resources to support the reliability and security of the electric
grid [1]. Te electrical power cables, underground and
overlines, suitably sustain the integration of diverse energy
resources, which must handle the volatility of the current
fow in the system. For the underground cable, the soil and
environmental conditions play a major role on the efcient
operation of the power cables. Terefore, there exists sub-
stantial research on the current rating of buried power cables
and methods used to eliminate soil drying out due to the
installed power cables [2]. Te most common and efective
method is to use engineered backfll material with low
thermal resistivity to backfll the cable trenches in which the
cables are buried [3, 4]. Te engineered backfll material has
been found to be expensive; hence, the use of South African
native soil is being investigated, including all soil properties,
such as thermal resistivity, composition, compaction and
moisture content. Another reliable solution is the forced
cooling method. Tis solution can be used provided there is
sufcient cooling resource. Te typical forced cooling sys-
tems include irrigation systems, separate pipe cooling,
concrete racks cooling and forced air cooling [5–7]. Te
problem with the forced cooling method is the amount of
water required to implement this solution. Te scarcity of
water in South Africa makes this solution not viable for
South African use.
Some designs of cables use superconductors [8, 9]. Tese
kinds of conductors use niobium or niobium-tin, which, at

Te use of innovative technologies is the future of

optic cable solutions to perform online monitoring and
diagnostic analysis of the electrical infrastructure, thereby
assisting utilities in planning their maintenance proactively
[11]. Te fbre optic cables can either be embedded in the
diferent layers of the cable, during its manufacturing
process, or they can be installed next to the power cable in
the same trench [3]. Te fbre optic cable will then be
connected to an online monitoring system, e.g., distributed
temperature sensing (DTS), conductor temperature moni-
toring technologies and dynamic current rating (DCR)
technologies [12]. It is, however, not feasible to install these
technologies in all MV and LV cable installations due to the
cost of these technologies. Te use of native soil for opti-
mised cable systems design, which limits soil drying out, was
investigated due to the cost of most of the technologies
available. In [13], a modifed algorithm strategy to deal with
underground cables that can minimise material costs for
high voltage (HV) networks is presented.
Besides, the majority of transmission and distribution
networks in South Africa are overhead networks. Te need
to construct and install underground cable systems has
increased due to the large-scale urban developments in
South Africa over the past 50 years [14]. Tus, South African
utilities must be equipped to design, install, operate and
maintain these underground cable systems. Furthermore,
the cable rating and design calculation methods [15] used for
installing these cable systems need to be considered. Tus,
this paper describes the procedure to be followed for
optimised underground power cable systems designs for the
consideration of soil drying out and dryer installation
condition environments where the South African native soil
will be used to backfll the cable tranches.
Soil drying out is a result of a moisture migration process
which begins when a temperature gradient is imposed across
the soil [16–18]. Tis temperature gradient will cause a water
vapour pressure gradient to develop, resulting in moisture
migrating away from the heat source. In this scenario, the
heat source would be the buried cable. Te moisture mi-
gration will eventually cause local drying of the soil near the
cable [17, 19]. Tus, the original cable rating calculations
[15], which would have been performed before the in-
stallation of the cable, would not be valid anymore since the
resistivity of the soil would have changed.
Based on the assumptions made to perform the cable
rating calculations, the calculated results for cable system
designs may lead to the underutilisation of the installed
expensive cable system infrastructures during operation by
the power utilities. IEC 60287 specifes the constants and
variables to be used for cable rating calculations [20–22].
Tis is mostly based on diferent temperature levels. Te
cable-specifc constants and variables are dependent on the

Tis paper describes the procedure to be followed for
a suitable current rating method that prevents soil drying out
by using the IEC 60287 calculation method on South African
native soil [25]. A contrast has been observed with the
existing techniques regarding the native soil and local cli-
mate, which have been underexplored in several prior works.
Terefore, a procedural guideline for the utilities is pre-
sented. Tis includes choosing or sizing the required cable
based on load requirements and available manufacturer’s
data sheets, performing soil thermal resistivity tests on the
selected cable route, determining cable laying depth
depending on the chosen cable route, and performing soil
ambient temperature measurement and cable laying depth.
Ten, the cable current rating calculations will be performed
to verify if the selected cable size will be sufcient for the
load; otherwise, a bigger cable size might need to be chosen.

3.1. Cable Rating in a Nonideal Cable Installation
Environment
3.1.1. Current Rating Based on the Change in Soil Termal
Resistivity. Te use of native soil to backfll cable trenches
will require that thermal resistivity tests be performed. For
this research, soil samples were taken from a known location
in South Africa (Sandton), where an electrical cable was
going to be installed. Te soil thermal resistivity measure-
ments were performed on the South African native soil at
diferent moisture content, which in turn resulted in dif-
ferent soil thermal resistivity. Table 2 presents the soil
thermal resistivity in the function of soil moisture. Te cable
current rating calculations, as shown in Table 3, were then
performed for the measured soil thermal resistivity de-
scribed in Table 2.
Te developed Excel calculating tool was used to cal-
culate the cable rating; only the soil thermal resistivity was
changed, and the calculated current rating was recorded in
Table 3. Table 3 shows the cable rating calculation results
based on diferent soil thermal resistivity. Te soil thermal
resistivity test results, as stated in Table 2, were used to
recalculate the current rating of the three diferent cables. It
is shown in Table 2 that the higher the soil moisture content,
the lower the thermal resistivity of the soil. Te best current
rating results were obtained when the soil thermal resistivity
was 0.596 K·m/W, and moisture content was 14.5%.
Te worst soil thermal resistivity at 0% moisture content
should be used for cable current rating to eliminate soil
drying out due to the installed power cables.
3.1.2. Cable Current Rating Based on Diferent Soil Ambient
Temperature. Te South African average soil ambient
temperature at about 850 mm below ground is about 25°C.
Te average soil ambient temperature is expected to increase
due to global warming and other factors, such as installed
services underground. Te more services installed un-
derground, the greater will be the soil ambient temperature,
and the more difcult it will be to dissipate heat. Te ideal
soil ambient temperature at about 850 mm below ground is
about 25°C. Tus, cable current rating was performed for
diferent soil ambient temperatures (Table 4), assuming that
the soil ambient temperature will be increasing over the next
few years. Te developed Excel calculating tool was used to
calculate the cable rating; only the soil ambient temperature
was changed, and the calculated current rating is recorded in
Table 4. Te best current rating results were obtained when
the soil ambient temperature was 24°C.
Te worst soil ambient temperature in Table 4 (28°C)
illustrates that the higher the soil ambient temperature, the
worse the cable current rating is. Terefore, it is recom-
mended that the soil ambient temperature at cable laying
depth should be measured and used in the cable current
rating calculations.

and animals could be compromised once the cable is ex-
posed to the public. Tus, power cables are predominately

5. Representation of Results
5.1. Results Based on Increased Soil Termal Resistivity.
Te thermal resistivity of the South African native soil is
expected to increase due to global warming and de-
sertifcation. Te desertifcation phase will result in the
migration of the moisture content in the soil; this, in turn,
will result in higher soil thermal resistivity. Te higher the
soil’s thermal resistivity, the more difcult it will be for the
soil to dissipate heat around the cable. An increase in the
conductor temperature will increase the surface temperature
of the cable; consequently, the soil closest to the cable will
solidify depending on the surface temperature of the cable.
Tus, the need to perform soil thermal resistivity mea-
surements before installing power cables is justifed. Te soil
thermal resistivity measurement performed on the soil
sample taken from the Sandton area (in South Africa)
proved the impact of change in moisture content in the soil.
Te measured soil thermal resistivity increased from
0.596 K·m/W when the soil moisture content was 14.5% to
3.720 K·m/W when the soil moisture content was 0%. Te
calculated average current rating due to the two diferent soil
thermal resistivity was 518.34 A and 224.21 A, respectively.
5.2. Results Based on Soil Ambient Temperature. Global
warming will further infuence the increase of soil ambient
temperature. Te average ambient temperature in South
Africa has been increasing by an average of 0.9°C [26] per
year for the past few years due to global warming; this
equates to an average increase of approximately 9°C every
10 years. Tis could result in a fraction of an increase in soil
ambient temperature. Tis, in turn, will have a negative
impact on the ampacity of buried cables.
Tables 4 and 8 show that the current rating calculations
reduce by an average of 4.27 A (1.12%) for a 1°C increase in
soil ambient temperature.
5.3. Results Based on the Change in Cable Laying Depth.
Te onsite installation conditions difer per project site.
Tere could be site conditions that would require that the
cable trench be dug deeper or shallower than the ideal cable
laying depth of 850 mm. Tese changes in cable laying depth
have a great impact on the cable ampacity rating. Te deeper
the cable is laid, the worse the cable ampacity rating.

\n5.6. Discussion. In this paper, it was shown that three major
installation conditions/parameters are to be considered
when performing current rating calculations for a single
circuit of buried cables; these parameters are the soil thermal
resistivity, including the soil moisture content, the cable
laying depth and the soil ambient temperature. Tese three
parameters are unfortunately not fxed as they can change
depending on the environment and installation condition.
It was shown in this paper that the best current rating for
buried cables is achieved when the soil is moist. Te more
humid the soil, the less is the soil thermal resistivity, and the
more current can be transmitted through a cable; thus, the
current rating is directly proportional to the percentage of
moisture content of the soil where the cable will be installed.
South Africa has been going through a desertifcation phase
due to global warming. Tis means that the design engineers
will have to be more conservative when designing and
selecting cable sizes for projects.
Te average ambient temperature has been increasing
over the years; this is directly proportional to the soil am-
bient temperature to which power cables are installed.
Utilities have standard designs for cable trenches for
diferent types of cable installations. However, it is not al-
ways possible to comply with the required installation
depths due to onsite conditions. Some installations would
require deeper cable trenches compared to the standard
design of cable trenches. Derating of the cable will then have
to be considered for these kinds of installations. Tis paper
has shown that the deeper the cable laying depth, the less the
current rating. Te best results were achieved when the cable
was laid in a shallow cable trench. Tis, however, cannot be
permitted as these cables would be alive, carrying current in
the range of hundreds of amps and the range of thousands of
volts; this is a safety hazard to the public and the
environment.
Te cable rating results deteriorated signifcantly when
all three nonideal parameters—soil thermal resistivity of
3.72 K·m/W, cable laying depth of 1150 mm and soil am-
bient temperature of 28°C—were simultaneously consid-
ered. Under these worst-case conditions, the calculated
current rating for Cable A dropped to 208.70 A, compared to
382.37 A under ideal conditions (1.2 K·m/W thermal re-
sistivity, 850 mm depth and 25°C soil temperature), resulting
in an average ampacity reduction of approximately 45.4%.
Tis reduction is primarily due to the infuence these pa-
rameters have on the thermal resistance to the surrounding
environment (denoted as T4 in the IEC 60287 standard).
According to the ampacity formulation, in Equation (1), the
allowable current I is inversely related to the square root of
the total thermal resistance, which includes T4. In this
circumstance, T4, the thermal resistance between the cable
surface and surrounding soil increases signifcantly with
higher soil thermal resistivity, greater burial depth and in-
creased ambient temperature.
For example, T4 increases from 0.712 K·m/W, at ideal
1.2 K·m/W soil resistivity, to over 1.186 K·m/W at 3.72 K·m/
W soil resistivity, as presented in Table 7. Tis increase in T4
directly raises the denominator of the ampacity formula,
reducing the current rating. Moreover, while ∆θ was held
constant at 45°C in all simulations to isolate the impact of T4,
the compounded efect of reduced heat dissipation (from
poor thermal conductivity), increased soil temperature and
deeper burial, which typically corresponds to denser, less
porous soil layers. Tis amplifes the thermal bottleneck
around the cable. Terefore, the study confrms that worst-
case environmental conditions must be assumed during the
design phase to avoid dangerously overestimating cable
ampacity. Tis also justifes the recommendation to use the
lowest practical soil moisture condition, considering 0%
moisture content, equating to the highest resistivity, for
sizing underground cables in semiarid regions such as South
Africa, as well as the incorporation of real-time monitoring
for instant DTS systems in critical installations. Besides, it
should be noted that the manufacturers’ data sheets shown
in Figure 1 were used to perform the calculated results, as
shown in Table 10.
It was found that the current rating of the cable is in-
versely proportional to T4, inversely proportional to the
increase in soil ambient temperature; however, it was found
that there is no mathematical relationship between the
changes in cable laying depth and the temperature rise of the
cable. Te temperature rise of the cable due to increased
cable depth can only be verifed by measuring the ambient
temperature at the cable laying depth. However, the prob-
ability of an increase in temperature rise is high when the
cable laying depth is increased. Tis, however, proves that
the thermal resistivity of the soil increases the deeper trench,
depending on moisture content, moisture content, soil
composition and layering and soil composition and layering.
Tis may be attributed to the compacted and denser nature
of deeper soil layers, which are often less porous and retain
less moisture—factors that collectively reduce the soil’s
ability to dissipate heat away from the buried cable.
Te fow diagram of the procedure, as shown in Figure 6,

rating calculations. Te tool can further be used to verify the
work done by consultants (where applicable).
Te use of DTS to monitor the surface temperature of the
cable outer sheath against the allowed maximum surface
temperature of the cable outer sheath would reduce the
probability of soil drying out due to the high surface tem-
perature of the cable outer sheath. Tis is because the DTS
will allow the utilities to proactively perform the necessary
switching to reduce the load on the afected cable networks.
Although there are available technologies [3, 12, 27, 28]

to backfll the cable tranches to minimise the capital cost for
the installation of underground cable systems.

6. Conclusion
Tis study has demonstrated that soil conditions, particu-
larly thermal resistivity, ambient temperature and cable
laying depth, signifcantly infuence underground cable
ampacity. Te experimental data collected from native South
African soil, specifcally from Sandton, revealed a wide range
of soil thermal resistivity values, from 0.596 K·m/W at 14.5%
moisture to 3.72 K·m/W at 0% moisture, with corresponding
current ratings decreasing from 518.34 A to 224.21 A. It was
shown that soil drying out drastically reduces cable per-
formance, and therefore, worst-case soil thermal resistivity
should be considered in the design to avoid long-term
degradation. In this study, the limitations of fndings are
based on measurements taken from a single soil type within
a specifc South African locality. Soil composition, moisture
retention, compaction and ambient temperature vary sig-
nifcantly across regions and climates. Terefore, the ap-
plicability of the proposed cable rating methodology and
Excel-based calculation tool may be limited in areas with
difering soil textures, e.g., sandy, clay, or loamy soils, dif-
ferent seasonal rainfall patterns, or cooler climates.
Furthermore, this work assumes constant environmental
parameters during operation, which are practically variable.
Besides, transient conditions, such as heavy rainfall, drought
or anthropogenic heat sources, e.g., nearby buried in-
frastructure, could alter soil properties over time. Terefore,
future research should explore soil thermal dynamics over
longer periods, assess various native soils from diferent
geographical zones and consider the efects of climate
change. It would also be valuable to integrate economic
assessments and real-time monitoring systems for adaptive
cable management strategies under uncertain and evolving
environmental conditions.
