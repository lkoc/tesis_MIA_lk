A B S T R A C T
Underground power cable systems (UPCSs) are generally buried close to the ground surface, exposing them to 
significant influences from ambient air and ground temperatures, which can affect heat dissipation and thermal 
efficiency. This study compares the heat transfer performance of UPCS with different cable bedding materials at 
critical current carrying capacity, considering the effects of ambient air and ground temperatures on system 
performance. The findings indicate that current carrying capacity decreases with higher ground temperatures, 
and that the critical ampacity leading to maximum cable temperature in UPCS is significantly influenced by 
actual ambient air and ground temperatures, rather than standard reference values like 20 ◦C. The newly 
developed cable bedding material, prepacked aggregate concrete (PAC), to enhance heat dissipation efficiency 
and prevent cable overheating is also proposed. Experimentally, PAC, with a higher thermal conductivity of 
2.094 W/(m⋅K) versus 1.365 W/(m⋅K) for sand, lowers the maximum cable temperature to 70.6 ◦C, compared to 
77.6 ◦C for sand under critical conditions. Moreover, the analytical solutions for ground temperature distribution 
models as boundary conditions are also highlighted, in which steady-state ground temperature analysis at the 
relevant depth may impact the accuracy of cable temperature predictions related to UPCS operation for both the 
system itself and the surrounding earth materials.
1. Introduction
Over the past few decades, conventional energy has been replaced 
with renewable energy, contributing to decarbonization and reducing 
greenhouse gas emissions and air pollution (Ritchie et al., 2024). More 
than 50 % of climate mitigation finance is allocated to implementing 
renewable energy production systems like hydropower, solar, and wind 
power (REN21, 2022). Among them, hydropower has the highest total 
generation, with a steady increase from about 1000 TWh in the early 
1960s to over 4000 TWh by 2023, accounting for over 60 % of renew­
able generation. Meanwhile, solar power shows the fastest growth, 
especially since 2010, although its total generation is just below 2000 
TWh by 2023 (Ritchie et al., 2024). Nevertheless, transmitting elec­
tricity over long distances, spanning several hundred to thousand kilo­
meters by overhead power transmission lines, remains a challenge. To 
install the overhead transmission lines, numerous electricity pylons 
should be constructed across rural and urban areas. However, overhead 
transmission cable is associated with poor esthetics and poses a safety 
risk to both animals and humans, particularly in densely populated 
areas. In addition, maintaining overhead cables is challenging due to 
their considerable height (i.e., at least 60 m) (Ahmad et al., 2019). To 
address these disadvantages, the underground power cable system 
(UPCS) has been recommended due to its safety, high life span, resulting 
in low maintenance costs in the long run, and potential to enhance the 
aesthetics of the city (Dai et al., 2014; Kroener et al., 2014; Ocło´n et al., 
2016). Notably, the cost of underground high-voltage cables is generally 
higher than that of overhead transmission lines due to the requirement 
for specialized installation techniques and materials (Arthur D. Little, 
Inc., 1977; Dinh et al., 2023; Metwally et al., 2013). However, because 
underground cables are protected by cable bedding materials, the 
negative effects arising from environmental factors such as extreme

Generally, the decision regarding the cable bedding material for 
UPCS is guided by multiple considerations, including thermal property, 
compaction, and material cost. A variety of conventional backfill and 
cable bedding materials for underground power cables have been 
studied and used in practice. These materials include mother soil, clay, 
sand, gravel, bentonite, lime, or its mixture with cement or other recy­
cled materials, such as fly ash and iron tailings (Gouda and El Dein, 
2015; Reddy et al., 2018; Song et al., 2019; Suleiman et al., 2020; Yi 
et al., 2023). Using recycled materials has several potential advantages, 
including reduced environmental impact and cost savings. However, the 
thermal conductivity of these materials is usually low and ranges from 
approximately 0.1 to 1.0 W/(m⋅K) (Dinh et al., 2023; Do et al., 2015; 
Suleiman et al., 2020). Additionally, using mother soil or sand as backfill 
material may lead to ground subsidence in the presence of heavy rainfall 
or during mechanical loading (i.e., vibration). Another cable bedding 
material for UPCS is normal concrete (NC), which is created by mixing 
all the components in advance, including coarse aggregates and mortar. 
The concrete uses large amounts of coarse aggregates, which generates 
the porosity in the formwork structure from 49−55 %. Particularly, 
coarse aggregates such as gravel and crushed rock have high thermal 
conductivity, ranging from 1.163 to 8.6 (W/(m. K)) (Khan, 2002; Zhang 
et al., 2015). Therefore, coarse aggregates are one of the main influ­
encing factors in the thermal properties of concrete. However, when 
installing an NC mixture in the trench with cable, the power cable sys­
tem may float under buoyancy force, resulting in the cable position 
deviating from that in the design (NS130 - Network Standard, 2020). To 
avoid buoyancy, cables should be installed with hold-downs for 
adequate anchoring, increasing the time and cost of installation.
The current carrying capacity is a crucial UPCS factor. The heat 
generated by the cable is significantly impacted by both the cable cur­
rent carrying capacity and electrical resistance. Consequently, numerous 
studies have been conducted to explore the impact of current carrying 
capacity and the efficiency of surrounding cable bedding on UPCS 
installation. These studies aim to mitigate the risk associated with cable 
overheating and to ensure cost-effectiveness throughout the phases of 
planning, design, and implementation (del Valle et al., 2008; Ocło´n 
et al., 2021; Venkata Rao, 2016). However, in the previous study, the 
electrical properties of cable in an underground cable system involved 
simplified assumptions with a single-core cable (de Lieto Vollaro et al., 
2014, 2011; Kroener et al., 2014; Salata et al., 2015; Vahidi and Mah­
moudi, 2012) or three single-core cables arranged in a flat or trefoil 
formation (Klimenta et al., 2018; Ocło´n et al., 2015a, 2015b; Quan et al., 
2019). In reality, the cable system in UPCS is complex with multiple 
layers and materials, which have a significant impact on the thermal 
performance and current carrying capacity of the cable. For instance, the 
thermal overlapping due to the multicable operation can increase the 
external thermal resistance and decrease the current carrying capacity. 
Consequently, the load loss rate used to determine the current carrying 
capacity should be reduced compared to using 100 %, as per the Inter­
national Electrotechnical Commission standard (IEC 60287-1-1, 2023), 
which may not accurately reflect actual operating conditions (Park 
et al., 2021).

Notably, UPCS is typically buried in the ground at a shallow depth 
ranging from 0.5 to 2.5 m (British Standard BS 7671, 2018; Kroener 
et al., 2014; Metwally et al., 2013; Salata et al., 2015), making it 
particularly susceptible to variations in ambient air and ground tem­
peratures and the solar radiation effect. The ground temperature in­
creases with the ambient air temperature, affecting the cable insulation, 
thermal expansion and contraction, and load capacity. Additionally, 
solar radiation mostly enters the soil to shallow depths, leading to a dry 
soil layer at less than 1 meter beneath the surface (Sun et al., 2023). 
However, high–voltage cables are usually buried 1.5 m deep as opposed 
to low-voltage cables, which are installed at depths of 0.7 m (Klimenta 
et al., 2018). Therefore, the thermal effect of solar radiation on 
high-voltage power cable systems is neglected and does not feature in 
IEC standards (IEC 60287-1-1, 2023; IEC TR 62095, 2003). In contrast, 
the temperature in the ground changes over time and depth due to 
variations in ambient air temperature, and this depends largely on the 
thermal diffusivity of the soil layers. Higher thermal diffusivity leads to a 
larger variable temperature zone. Regarding the ambient air tempera­
ture effect, the previous studies frequently assumed a constant temper­
ature for the ground surface (Hwang and Jiang, 2003; Mitchell and 
Abdel–Hadi, 1979; Ocło´n et al., 2015a; Quan et al., 2019), without 
considering realistic conditions such as forced convection terms gener­
ated by wind and seasonal variations. Furthermore, the other bound­
aries (left, right, and bottom edges) were assumed unknown values and 
imposed by adiabatic conditions, neglecting the actual ground temper­
ature distribution at the depth of cable burial.
In this study, prepacked aggregate concrete (PAC) is used as cable 
bedding material for UPCS, compared to sand and normal concrete. The 
main properties of PAC are high thermal conductivity with high grout 
flowability, which can enhance the heat transfer performance of UPCS 
during operation (Dinh et al., 2023). The thermal properties of cable 
bedding materials are also utilized in calculating the external thermal 
resistance, which in turn helps determine electrical characteristics. 
Additionally, current carrying capacity is determined by the distribution 
effect of multiple cables on each other, load loss rate factor, and thermal 
resistances, among other factors. The thermal resistances of XLPE 
insulation, external cable bedding material, and soil significantly affect 
the cable current carrying capacity. Over 70 % of the temperature in­
crease experienced by the conductor is attributed to external thermal 
resistance (Anders, 2005; Atoccsa et al., 2024; de Le´on and Anders, 
2008). To avoid thermal overlapping around the cable during operation, 
the load loss rate under real-world conditions is also presented. Based on 
the Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO) design standard, a load 
loss rate of 80 % should be applied in Korea (Park et al., 2021). Besides, 
the realistic cable−surrounding soil temperature is examined to deter­
mine the current carrying capacity based on the ground temperature 
distribution function. As a result, the ground temperature profile tends 
to converge to a constant value of 15.2 ◦C at a depth greater than 10 m, 
thereby imposing the constant temperature, T = 15.2 ◦C on the bottom 
edge for the computational boundary condition.
This study aims to demonstrate the advantages of utilizing prepacked 
aggregate concrete as a cable bedding material in Underground Power 
Cable Systems (UPCS). Improved thermal properties, and a positive 
impact of PAC on the current carrying capacity of the cables, are brought 
out by the analysis. The paper provides a detailed analysis of thermal 
and electrical characteristics influenced by ambient air and ground 
temperatures. It thus underlines the criticality of the ambient factors and 
cable bedding materials during the operation of a UPCS. The structure of 
this paper is organized as follows: Section 2 discusses the mathematical 
model of underground power cable systems, including the governing 
equations and determination of the electrical characteristics of the 
cable. Section 3 presents the boundary conditions concerning ground 
temperature distribution, grid independence test, and computational 
domain. Section 4 discusses the general characteristics of the materials 
used. Section 5 gives the results and discussions. Section 6 concludes the 
work.

3.2. Determination of computational domain
In this study, based on an analytical solution of ground temperature 
distribution under the effect of ambient air temperature, the sides and 
bottom boundaries are imposed as temperature profiles with depth and 
time T(z, t) and constant ground temperature of 15.2 ◦C, respectively. 
The computational domain determination procedure is proposed and 
described in previous studies (Klimenta et al., 2014; Kocar and Ertas, 
2004). The domain dimensions should be defined to ensure that both the 
analytical solution and adiabatic conditions are satisfied, with the 
temperature gradient at the boundary being zero. The maximum tem­
perature of cable conductors with varying distances of boundaries, Db, 
are evaluated to determine the domain dimensions as shown in Fig. 8.

In the finite element method for modeling underground power ca­

and other physical properties exhibit detailed variations. Larger ele­

Normal concrete (NC) and prepacked aggregate concrete (PAC) were

first stage was packing coarse aggregates like gravel or others in 
designed formwork (Fig. 12). The second stage involved filling the high-

ground blast furnace slag powder (BFS), fly ash, crushed sand, and 
gravel. The grained size of sand was distributed from 0.15 mm to 5 mm 
and the fineness modulus was 2.45. The maximum size of gravel was 25 
mm, as in Fig. 12. The OPC met the standards of Korea and America like 
Korean Standard (KS) L 5201 Type I, ASTM C 595 Type I (SM), and CEM 
II/B-S 42.5. Other binder properties like fly ash and BFS and aggregates 
properties are listed in Table 7.
For the PAC, crushed gravel and fly ash were used, similar to in NC. 
However, the commercialized ultra-rapid-setting cement (URSC) and 
quartz sand with particle sizes ranging from 0.075 mm to 0.22 mm were 
used to replace the OPC and crushed sand. In this implementation, the 
PAC mixture was achieved by gravity method pouring, that is, the high 
flowability grout was downward poured in the void by its weight. The 
mix proportions of NC and PAC mixtures can be found in Table 8. The 
thermal properties of traditional cable bedding, and natural sand, were 
determined using the KD2 Pro device provided by Decagon Devices. 
Meanwhile, Hot Disk M1 equipment using a hot disk sensor with model 
8563 was conducted for NC and PAC samples (Fig. 13). Table 9 lists the 
measurement results of the general properties of samples. The thermal 
conductivity of NC and PAC were nearly identical–2.093 and 2.094 W/ 
(m.K), respectively–and were 35 % higher than that of sand at 1.365 W/ 
(m.K). Moreover, to avoid the effects of mechanical loads (vibrations) on 
the polyethylene casing pipes, controlled low-strength material (CLSM) 
was used to fill the air gap space between the casing pipe and power 
cable, as shown in Fig. 1. CLSM from a previous study was investigated 
using typical raw steelmaking slag and ground steelmaking slag to 
replace natural sand in a CLSM mixture with 10, 20, and 30 % volume 
fractions (Dinh et al., 2020). Due to its high flowability, high thermal 
conductivity, and low bleeding rate, CLSM can improve the heat transfer 
process from a power cable to the surrounding cable bedding and pro­
vide mechanical support to protect the cable. The general properties of

5.1. Alternating current carrying capacity and volumetric power of heat

The current carrying capacity is calculated from Eq. (3), as per 
standards, including the International Electrotechnical Commission 
(IEC) - 60287 and Korea Electric Power Corporation (KEPCO) DS-6210. 
Eq. (3) indicates that the current carrying capacity values are mainly a 
function of temperature, ΔT, and thermal resistances.
This study considers the multipipe cables laying on cable bedding 
embedded in natural soil. Therefore, the thermal resistance should 
consider the mutual heating of multipipe cables according to the cable 
distances (IEC 60287-2-1, 2023; Park et al., 2021). In the IEC 60287–2–1 
standard, the external resistance is explained based on the mutual 
thermal effect of multipipe cables; however, the load loss factor was not 
considered for calculation, that is, 100 % load was applied. To prevent 
overheating under actual operating conditions, the calculated thermal 
resistance should be reduced by applying a load loss factor, Lf, as pre­
sented in the previous section. During heat generation from the cable 
under actual operation conditions, thermal superposition occurs in the 
soil surrounding the cable. This thermal overlap leads to an actual soil 
thermal resistance that is higher than the value calculated based solely

effective heat dissipation in NC and PAC, the cable experiences rela­
tively lower temperatures, resulting in safe operating conditions.

bedding materials. The results indicate that the maximum temperature 
of the top layer cable conductor is lower than that of the bottom cable 
conductor. The primary cause is the presence of forced convection 
boundary conditions, which act as coolers in relation to the underground 
power cables. Although the ambient air temperature on the ground 
surface is higher than the ground temperature at the bottom boundary 
during the summer, the temperature gradient from the cable conductor 
to the ground surface is larger than that to the bottom edge due to the 
shallow depth of the cable installation. Consequently, the temperature 
in the top layer cables tends to dissipate towards the surface, enabling 
the cooling of the conductor temperature. This cooling effect signifi­
cantly impacts the temperature difference between the top and bottom 
cable-layers. The difference in temperature increases with an increase in 
thermal conductivity (ΔT = TCmax, B1 −TCmax, T1 = 1.0 ∘C for sand and 
1.4 ◦C for NC and PAC (summer season)) and ΔT = 1.3 ◦C for sand and 
1.6 ◦C for PAC and NC in the winter season. Moreover, the maximum 
temperature of the central cable conductors was higher than that of the

for PAC and NC, whereas it reduces to 57.1 ◦C for sand and 49.9 ◦C for 
NC and PAC in winter conditions.

6. Conclusion
In this study, the effect of the ambient air and ground temperatures 
on the heat transfer of a UPCS is investigated using a finite element 
program (COMSOL Multiphysics). The current carrying capacity is 
calculated by considering the influences of temperature changes, multi- 
cable structure, thermal properties of cable bedding materials, and load 
loss rate factor. The prepacked aggregate concrete (PAC) has been 
introduced as a cable bedding material to address the drawbacks of 
using normal concrete (NC) and natural sand. The numerical heat 
transfer simulations of underground power cable system (UPCS) are 
analyzed, and the following conclusions are obtained:
i. The current carrying capacity is a main function of the ambient 
temperatures and thermal properties of the environment sur­
rounding the cable. The results indicate that as analytical ground 
temperatures rise, the current carrying capacity decreases. In 
addition, the critical ampacity that leads to the maximum cable 
temperature in UPCS is significantly affected by real-world con­
ditions of ambient air and ground temperatures, rather than by 
considering standard reference values like 20 ◦C.
ii. Applying the analytical solution with boundary conditions T(z, t)
and Tg for realistic modeling shows a significant temperature 
decrease by approximately 14.5 ◦C in the bottom region and 
around 1.6 ◦C in the conductor. In addition, a reduction in 
ambient air temperature from summer to winter can reduce the 
maximum cable temperature by nearly 10 %.
iii. Prepacked aggregate concrete (PAC) is proposed as cable bedding 
material, constructed via two-stage packing using high flow­
ability grout to address the drawback of normal concrete 
bedding. PAC offers a high thermal conductivity of 2.094 W/ 
(m⋅K), compared to sand of 1.365 W/(m⋅K), resulting in reduced 
external thermal resistance, increased current carrying capacity, 
and improved heat dissipation from UPCS. Additionally, the 
higher thermal conductivity of PAC lowers the maximum cable 
temperature to 70.6 ◦C, compared to 77.6 ◦C with sand in the hot 
season, and 49.9 ◦C in the cold season, versus 57.1 ◦C for sand.
iv. The paper presents PAC bedding material that combines natural 
aggregates—such as gravel—with high thermal conductivity, 
aiming to improve UPCS performance. Even though natural ag­
gregates may increase the investment cost, the feasibility of using 
recycled aggregates, such as recycled concrete or steel-making 
slag, is being explored as a cost-effective alternative. The goal 
is to develop guidelines for using recycled materials in UPCS, 
improving both economic and environmental outcomes.
